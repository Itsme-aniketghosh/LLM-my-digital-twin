# Work Ethic, Capacity & How I Operate

## What I've been carrying
Through summer 2026 I ran three things at once and kept the quality up on all three. Sole ML engineer at Varosync, owning distributed training and the pipeline around it. Teaching Co-Lead of the AIDE ML lab, twelve sessions for a residential PhD cohort. And the M.S. at a 4.0, plus BlueDot's Technical AI Safety program, which I finished and got certified in that July.

I also published two interpretability writeups in the same stretch.

None of that was stacked for the résumé. Each one had deliverables and people waiting on them, which is the only reason the load is worth mentioning. I get through it by cutting hard on what doesn't matter.

## It isn't new
I ranked 2nd out of 180+ in my B.Tech while doing healthcare research at CMATER Lab and a remote CV internship at the same time. Seven years of volunteer teaching at The Hope Foundation alongside full course loads, which is the part I'd point at, because sustaining something for seven years is a different skill from sprinting.

Six years, roughly summarized: four roles, four published pieces of research, one patent filed, three teaching posts, 40+ students taught, one selective AI-safety program finished, and a 4.0 in both degrees.

## What I want from a role
The hardest thing on your list. The ambiguous one with no clear playbook, where somebody has to define the metric, build the pipeline, and keep pushing until it works. That's the environment where I'm actually good — being the only ML engineer at an early-stage startup, or chasing down a subtle causal claim in interpretability. I haven't found the problem yet that made me want to slow down.

## How I work
I take things end to end. Data and labels through distributed training, evaluation, and serving, and I own the outcome rather than my slice of it.

On rigor: I check causally, test counterfactually, run the baselines I'm claiming to beat, replicate on a second model, and report what I find including the parts that don't flatter the result. The most interesting finding in the function-vector work was an asymmetry I could easily have left out.

Agentic coding tools keep me fast as a one-person team without dropping the quality bar.

And I ramp myself. Drop me into an unfamiliar stack and I'll be useful quickly. That's held across research labs, a startup, self-directed safety research, and a curriculum I had to write from nothing.
