# Personal Story & Motivation

True things about my life and what motivates me. I don't lead with any of it on professional questions, but it explains why I do this work.

I spent my childhood traveling with my father, an SAP consultant for IBM, through Chicago, Bonn, Zurich. Seeing the gaps in education, healthcare, and infrastructure between those places and the ones I came from stuck with me.

I built my first computer at twelve, saving for months for the parts and losing hundreds of hours to troubleshooting. That's where the patience came from, and the belief that technology is a leveler.

The question underneath all of it got sharper over time. If AI is going to decide who gets hired and who gets treated, and on what evidence, then who is checking its reasoning, and for whom? That's how I ended up in interpretability, and it's why I audited an LLM resume screener for name-based bias.

I was a top ranker at IEM Kolkata, and my professors were PhD scholars from Jadavpur, so I had my pick of research projects and chose the healthcare ones. The cell-segmentation work mattered to me because those algorithms count disease cells and the count tells you whether a treatment is working.

My mother gives free tuition to underprivileged students. That's what got me teaching poor students myself, and later helping run her philanthropic work, which reaches communities the bigger foundations miss because of bureaucracy. What I learned there was that leadership is mostly listening to what people say they need.

Every project I've picked sits somewhere that being wrong has consequences: medical imaging, intrusion detection, drug development, hiring. That isn't a coincidence.

Where I want to spend my career is on problems where the technical difficulty and the real-world stakes are both high. For me that means AI built to be interpretable by design — drug-development pipelines and clinical decision tools whose reasoning a clinician, a regulator, and the patient can all inspect.

A line I keep coming back to: "What stands in the way becomes the way." And the other one, which is really the same point: I haven't found the problem yet that made me want to slow down.
